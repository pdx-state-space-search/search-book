## Week 9 findings

### W9-1 — solved values and exact fixed horizons

**Must fix technical; definite wording/specification defect.**

Anchor: `src/09-adversarial.md:276`, especially lines 279–281;
compare the truncated-tree definition at lines 169–173.

The chapter says that preserving an exact fixed-horizon
specification permits either matching-horizon values or a
genuinely solved full-game value. The second alternative
does not follow. A solved full-game value can differ from
the value of the specified truncated tree just as a deeper
heuristic result can.

Counterexample: let a nonterminal MIN state have two terminal
MAX-win children, both with utility +1, and let its static
evaluation be E(s)=0. The solved value is +1. Its horizon-zero
value under the stated rule is 0. Returning the solved value
changes that fixed-horizon function. To avoid horizon zero,
put this state immediately below a one-action MAX root:
depth-one minimax returns 0, whereas using the solved entry
returns +1. Both trees are finite and satisfy the chapter's
game assumptions.

Repair: retain the matching-horizon/compatible-semantics rule
for reproducing the exact truncated function. Explain that
full-game solved entries can deliberately supersede static
evaluation, but that this defines stronger, different leaf
semantics. Alternatively define the fixed-horizon function
from the outset to evaluate genuinely solved states before
the horizon test, and make that policy part of compatibility.
The distinction is about specifications, not a claim that
using a solved result is bad game-engine practice.

### W9-6 — complexity wording can identify the counted work

**Optional precision.**

Anchor: `src/09-adversarial.md:87`–95 and :144–147.

Full-tree O(b^D), incremental-stack O(D), and stored-sibling
O(bD) are appropriate under the stated representation model.
Say explicitly that the time expression counts tree records
or leaf evaluations with bounded per-record work; move
generation, copying, and evaluation may have meaningful cost.

The ideal alpha-beta b^(D/2) shorthand is conventional when
b is regarded as fixed. If treating b and D as independently
variable, odd depths need care. The standard perfect-order
leaf count on a uniform depth-D b-ary tree is
b^ceil(D/2)+b^floor(D/2)−1. For b=4, depths 1, 3, 4 require
4, 19, 31 leaves. The chapter says "on the order of," not an
exact two-parameter theorem, so this is a refinement rather
than a major mathematical error. State fixed b, or give the
ceil/floor expression and explain the roughly half-depth
exponent informally.

## Week 10 findings

### W10-1 — explain LaCAM*'s objective qualification

**Substantive teaching; qualified claim is technically sound.**

Anchors: `src/10-frontiers.md:259`–286, especially :281–284.

The paper qualification "accumulated-transition-cost model"
is accurate, but not intelligible from this chapter alone.
Immediately beforehand the book introduces makespan and sum
of costs and says the joint state records one location per
agent. Readers could reasonably assume the cited guarantee
automatically applies to either objective on that state.

The paper explicitly distinguishes ordinary flowtime/sum of
costs, which can depend on earlier goal visits, from a cost
summed over location-configuration transitions. It uses
sum-of-loss and makespan; augmented goal semantics can change
what is representable. See §2.1, p. 244 of
[Okumura's IJCAI paper](https://ijcai.org/proceedings/2023/0028.pdf).
This was a selected-passage check, not a full-paper review.

Repair: define the qualification concretely: charging one
per joint time step yields makespan. Explain that the usual
sum of arrival times needs compatible state/arrival rules
and must not be inferred from the theorem's name. There is
no need to teach every MAPF objective; one positive example
and one explicit boundary would make the condition useful.
Keep the existing warning that eventual optimality supplies
no practical deadline.

### W10-2 — an end-to-end bidirectional trace is missing

**Substantive teaching; no counterexample to the stated rule.**

Anchor: `src/10-frontiers.md:118`–175.

The first-meeting example correctly produces 101 and 100.
It teaches a wrong stopping rule, but never demonstrates
the right algorithm's two queues, labels, joins, direction
selection, and final m_f+m_b comparison. The four-step
discipline says "relevant crossing edges" without giving
the concrete update a reader should perform. "Settled"
first appears in the proof, rather than being defined here
as a vertex removed nonstale at minimum distance and
finalized by Dijkstra's rule.

Repair: retain the four-vertex example, draw its directed
edges, choose a direction policy and ties, and display
several queue minima and μ updates until the stopping
comparison proves 100. Give the symmetric crossing-edge
update, for example g_f(u)+c(u,v)+g_b(v), whenever both
labels are finite. State that every valid discovered join
is an upper bound and that the implementation must check
all required joins even if a relaxation does not improve
the same-direction label. Define settled and distinguish
it from merely discovered. State a finite-graph setting
for the terminating algorithm, or explicitly inherit the
earlier infinite-space progress conditions.

The stopping argument agrees with Goldberg's primary-source
treatment; no technical defect was found in the discipline
as written, assuming its promised joining invariant holds.
[Goldberg, §2, pp. 2–3](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/2016/02/goldberg-sofsem07.pdf)
supports the paired-minimum stopping condition and the
warning that a meeting vertex need not be on the optimum.
